An Unexplained Score Made People Doubt the Advice

In two weeks, I tested an AI resume tool’s prototype with six job seekers and found the score was undercutting the AI’s advice. The team rebuilt the flow before engineering started.

TL;DR

What was at stake
The client was betting that AI feedback would make the tool worth using for job seekers.
What changed
The team rebuilt the flow around specific recommendations with AI-generated before-and-after examples, and moved the score to a secondary, explained role.
My role
Solo researcher and strategist, from study design through recommendations
Timeline
About 2 weeks to recommendations, then a readout and follow-up test
Measurable outcome
$50K–$65K

Engineering cost avoided. A four-person team for two sprints is about 640 hours, at fully loaded salary rates.

5 of 6

Participants more confident they could revise their resume in the follow-up test, measured on the same pre and post scale.

Rebuilt

Recommendations first, with AI before-and-after examples, and the score moved below them with an explanation.

Aha moment

An unexplained score made users skeptical of the advice that came with it.

Read the full case study
Recommendations

Three changes to the flow

Lead with specific recommendations

Tie each suggestion to a line in the person’s resume and say what to change, so the next step is obvious.

Show before-and-after examples

Have the AI rewrite the person’s own line as an example, so they can see what “better” looks like.

Make the score secondary and explain it

Move it below the recommendations and show what it measures, so it reads as context.

The tension

The team wanted to keep the score because it gave people something concrete to watch go up, or down, as they made changes. I’d have tested dropping it. It stayed, explained, and moved below the advice.

The hypothesis

Two views of the score

The team’s hypothesis

A score up front would make people want to raise it, so they’d engage with the feedback.

What the research showedNot supported

People fixated on the number. When it felt arbitrary, they did not trust the suggestions under it.

My hypothesis

The tool might not need a score at all.

What would have changed my mindIf people had used the score as a target and revised more, I’d have dropped the idea of cutting it.

What the research showedPartly supported

The score stayed, but only as context below the recommendations.

The challenge

Two sprints from build

Two engineers were already choosing parsing libraries and testing prompts for a design built around the score.

The tool analyzed a resume and suggested improvements. The prototype was a high-fidelity Figma flow with simulated AI output: upload a resume, read the feedback, see a score. I came in when the team was fairly sure what they wanted to build, but before engineering had committed to building it.

Approach

A small test aimed at one decision

Six target users uploaded their own resumes into the clickable prototype. I measured whether each person could revise their resume using the feedback, and how confident they felt doing it, before and after.

Six people was enough to see the pattern without spending extra time on more sessions.

Findings

The score got the attention the feedback needed

People fixated on the score

All 6 participants looked at the number first, and the recommendations got less of their attention.

An arbitrary-feeling score broke trust

Four of the six said the score felt arbitrary. With no explanation of how it was calculated, the score made people doubt the rest of the feedback too.

General suggestions weren’t actionable

People didn’t know what to change when a suggestion wasn’t tied to a specific line.

Reflection

Impact

This project changed how I think about scores in AI products. The number looked authoritative, but there was no explanation for how it was calculated, and people stopped trusting the advice underneath it. For the team, this meant building the recommendation-first version from the start, instead of reworking a score-first one after launch.

Hindsight

1
Measure the revisions, too

Confidence was self-reported. Having someone rate the before and after resumes would show whether people’s revisions actually got better.I could say the redesign produced better resumes, not only more confident users.

2
Check the score against expert ratings

Participants judged whether the score felt fair. Running it against recruiter ratings with my calibration audit would show whether a high score actually meant a strong resume.The team would have known whether to fix the score or drop it, instead of only moving it down the page.

Next steps

With two more weeks

1
Test the score explanation on its own

See whether showing how the score is calculated builds trust, or whether the score should go entirely.

2
Watch real use after launch

Track whether people come back for a second round of revisions, which a one-session test can’t show.
