Why a Product People Liked Still Felt “Not for Me”

Usability and go-to-market research for an early-stage AI health platform that people liked but weren’t signing up for

TL;DR

What changed
The founders added employer and union channels alongside direct-to-consumer, and started re-engagement emails for trial users who never converted. Outreach to advocacy groups such as NAACP was underway when the engagement ended.
Result
The new channel strategy led directly to the platform’s first six-figure service agreement and to growth from a few users to more than 16,000, past the 10,000-user target.
My role
Solo researcher and strategist for the founders, from study design and recruiting through sessions, behavioral analytics, and recommendations
Timeline
About 3.5 months: 6 weeks of research through the January readout, then advisory through March
Why it mattered
The company needed to grow past friends and early adopters to a real user base before its runway ran out.
Aha moment

The founders wanted personas so they could target their ads. Partway through the research, I realized sharper targeting wouldn’t fix anything, because people either didn’t trust the product or didn’t see it as something for them.

Recommendations

Meet people through someone they already trust

The Trust-Transfer Model introduces the product through an institution people already trust, like their employer or union.

Primary
Recommendation
Offer it through employers and unions

Partner with self-funded employers, stop-loss carriers, unions, and other trusted groups to offer the product as a benefit. Start where the value is easy for the employer to see: burnout among healthcare workers, time lost by professionals caring for aging parents, and absenteeism in manufacturing.

Complementary
Channel
Borrow trust from advocacy groups

Seek endorsements from groups like the Alzheimer’s Association, caregiver alliances, AARP, and NAACP health initiatives, so people who find the product on their own see a name they recognize.

Validation
Before Scale
Run a 30-day pilot first

Test the partner approach with a 30-day pilot before shifting the whole go-to-market plan, with a clear point to stop if it didn’t reduce the friction I saw in research.

Retention
Quick Win
Follow up with trial users

Behavioral analytics showed no follow-up with people who started a free trial and didn’t subscribe. I recommended a short re-engagement email sequence for them, since the cost of reaching them had already been spent.

The hypothesis

The founders saw a product problem

The founders’ hypothesis

Adoption is a product or feature problem.

What the research showedNot supported

The interface had a few rough spots, but nothing that explained the drop-off. People were leaving at the entry point.

My hypothesis

People who backed out of participating had something to add, even without trying the product.

What the research showedSupported

The reasons people gave for backing out became the trust finding.

The challenge

A product that worked and a growth problem

The platform had only a few users, mostly friends and early adopters.

The client was an early-stage AI health platform built to work like a personal medical command center: one place for your health history, appointment prep, and a check on possible diagnostic errors. The technology worked and had early clinical backing. Getting people to sign up and stay was the problem.

I was brought in to run usability research and find out where people were dropping off. I worked directly with the founders, with no PM or design team in between, and presented findings that challenged the go-to-market plan they had built.

Approach

Following the people who said no

I recruited and screened everyone myself, across a range of ages, health engagement, and comfort with technology, and ran the sessions over Zoom.

1
Screener survey
Recruited and qualified participants independently across varied health engagement profiles
2
Think-aloud usability
8 moderated sessions observing real-time navigation and verbal reasoning through the product
3
Semi-structured interviews
Post-session interviews exploring attitudes toward health data, AI, and personal health management
4
Non-completer analysis
Conversations with people who refused to try the product
5
Behavioral analytics
Analyzed site and in-product behavior to find drop-off points and check them against what people said
AI in the workflow

More than a week saved in a six-week study

3–4 daysTranscripts

Sessions were transcribed automatically. I exported the transcripts, converted them to .vtt files, and loaded them into Dovetail the same day. The automated transcripts had errors, so I checked each one against its recording before coding.

~2 daysCoding

Dovetail’s AI clustering did the first pass at grouping themes across sessions, which cut coding time about in half.

~1 dayReadout

Notion AI drafted slide copy from my theme notes, and I spent the saved day on the charts.

The rest of the saved time went into checking what people said in sessions against what the behavioral data showed.

Findings

People liked it. Most decided it wasn’t for them.

Behavioral analytics and interviews indicated the same thing. The bigger findings were about whether people saw the product as something for them.

1
The “Not for Me” Paradox

Most named someone else it would help: a parent, a grandparent, a friend with a chronic illness. Some said they would use it if their doctor told them to. That’s optimism bias, the belief that health risks apply more to other people, and I named it the “Not for Me” Paradox so the founders had a handle for it.

2
Compliance badges didn’t build trust

The people who backed out had already heard the product was HIPAA and SOC 2 compliant. For them, feeling safe was something the product hadn’t earned yet, and a badge on the page couldn’t supply it.

What it meant for growth
Selling direct to consumers would mean paying to overcome distrust and optimism bias one person at a time.
Reflection

Impact

This was the first time I delivered something other than what a client asked for. Partway through building personas, I could see they wouldn’t solve the founders’ problem, so I brought them a channel strategy instead, knowing it might not be welcome. For the founders, it replaced guesswork about who to target with a clear reason people weren’t signing up.

Hindsight

1
Test onboarding on its own

The research pointed to the entry point as the problem, but the sessions covered the whole product. A study built only around first-time activation would have shown exactly where trust broke down.

2
Push for one interface change to measure

The founders’ changes were about channels, not the interface, so there was nothing new to bring back into testing. I would push for one onboarding change alongside the strategy work, so a follow-up study could measure the result.

3
Recruit caregivers from the start

Caregivers came up during analysis as a strong segment, but I hadn’t recruited them on purpose. Building them into the screener from day one would have given that group its own findings and likely sped up the partner recommendation.

Next steps

With two more weeks

1
Study caregivers on their own

Recruit caregivers directly and give them their own sessions.

2
Test the pitch with benefits buyers

Employers decide through HR and benefits teams, so the partner pitch needs testing with them too.
