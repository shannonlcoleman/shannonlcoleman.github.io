Redefining What “Working IT” Means Across the Air Force

User research and service strategy for EITaaS, the Air Force’s move from base-by-base IT to one enterprise service for more than 800,000 personnel.

TL;DR

What changed
My five personas and their Experience Level Agreements went into the Wave 1 and Wave 2 solicitation requirements for a $5.7B contract.
My role
Research lead and advisor to Air Force program leadership, leading a team of about 4
Timeline
About 15 months
Why it mattered
The Air Force was about to commit to one enterprise IT contract. Without a definition of good service that users would recognize, the contract would only pay for uptime.
Recognition
A challenge coin from the U.S. Air Force Chief Experience Officer, for leading the move to a user-centered service model
Read the full case study
Recommendations

Three recommendations

Add Experience Level Agreements to the contract

Each persona got its own measures of success, written as a layer alongside the existing service levels rather than in place of them. Keeping the service levels gave procurement something familiar to build on.

Match hardware and support to the work

Stop buying the same equipment for everyone. Garrison Users get reliable, lower-cost devices. Power Users and Flightline Users get what their jobs require.

Tier the service desk

Route routine requests like password resets to self-service, measured by first contact resolution, and send complex issues to specialists with their own repair-time commitments. I helped set up this model.

Going in

The program measured whether the systems worked

The program’s hypothesis

If the systems met their service levels, the IT experience was good.

What the research showedNot supported

Even after the network upgrades at Buckley, 88% of users still reported at least one issue that hurt their work.

My hypothesis

One standard setup and support model can’t serve people working in very different conditions.

What the research showedSupported

Interviews at all nine installations sorted people into five groups with different needs, from flightline crews working offline to analysts who needed heavy compute.

The situation

Every base ran its own IT

Things were bad enough that an Air Force officer’s open letter, “Fix our computers,” went viral. Airmen and Guardians recognized the hour-long logins and ten forced restarts in a day he described, and the Air Force’s CIO answered him publicly.

More than 230 organizations could make changes on the network, and more than 80 separate authority-to-operate boundaries split it up. Every base bought its own hardware, ran its own helpdesk, and decided for itself what “working IT” meant. EITaaS, Enterprise IT as a Service, was meant to replace that with one commercial enterprise service. A pilot at nine installations tested the model before the Air Force committed to a $5.7B contract.

Isobar wasn’t part of the delivery team. We advised Air Force program leadership under the Architecture and Integration Directorate, so any influence had to come from the quality of the work. I joined mid-pilot, replacing the previous research lead.

Approach

Research that had to hold up in a contract

Describing the current experience wasn’t enough. The future state had to be specific enough for vendors to be measured against, so every finding needed to end in something countable.

  • Interviews across all nine installations, with Airmen, Guardians, and IT staff
  • Pre and post surveys, with a base outside the program as a control, weighted by status, role, and career family
  • Moderated usability testing of the enterprise service desk
  • Journey mapping from the moment something breaks to when it’s fixed

No XLA could be written until the personas existed, so interviews and personas took most of the first seven months. A bid protest paused the work for a few months in early 2023, and from month 10 Advana took more of my time.

Findings

Five kinds of users, five definitions of working IT

A desk-bound administrator and a flightline maintenance tech need different things from IT, work in different conditions, and mean different things when they say it works. Treating everyone as one user type was behind both the cost problem and the experience problem. The interviews showed what each group needed to do its job, and I turned those needs into thresholds the vendors could be held to.

Across the pilot bases, the program recorded 84% faster startup, 59% faster data transfer, and a 92% cut in security event workload. The Flightline XLA was written against those benchmarks.

Reading the survey results carefully

Satisfaction after a migration is easy to over-read. After the first network migration at Offutt, the share of users satisfied with the network fell from 28% to 15%. Buckley barely moved, and the control base held flat. Without the control and the weighting, that drop could have been blamed on survey timing or on who happened to answer. With them, it pointed at the migration. After the base network and Wi-Fi upgrades, Offutt recovered 14 points and Buckley rose 24.

Surveys only caught part of it. Responses were linked to service desk tickets and endpoint monitoring, which showed what the scores couldn’t. In earlier program data, 87% of incidents were resolved, but 52% took seven days or more.

That second answer is a user experience problem and a security problem at once. When access gets in the way of the job, people route around it, and no satisfaction score would have shown it.

What I’d do differently

Three changes in hindsight

1
Write down how each XLA was derived

I’d document the chain from specific interview and survey findings to each threshold. That would have made the framework easier to defend and to hand to the next phase of the program.

2
Build in a way to revisit the thresholds

The XLAs came from one point in time. A standing feedback loop would show whether a threshold was still the right thing to measure, and not only whether vendors were meeting it.

3
Invest in key relationships sooner

Our influence depended on trust with program leadership and on the Air Force survey office, which controlled when and how surveys went out. Earlier effort on both would have cut weeks of waiting.

Next steps

With two more weeks

1
Test an XLA against the survey data

Check whether hitting a threshold, like startup time for Flightline Users, actually tracks with that group’s satisfaction.

2
Dig into the core-services bottleneck

The survey report flagged DNS and the security layer as a likely bottleneck that still needed analysis.

3
Size the workaround problem

Find out how common it is for people to email work home to get around blocked sites.
